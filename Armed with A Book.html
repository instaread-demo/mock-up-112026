Welcome friend! Let’s continue the conversation about dopamine that I started in my previous post about The Dopamine Code. Why do we keep chasing the next thing, even when we haven’t taken the time to appreciate what we already have? And why can something as ordinary as checking email or scrolling through social media become such a persistent habit? In The Dopamine Code, neuroscientist and author Dr. Sydney Ceruto explores the role dopamine plays in our motivations, decisions, and everyday behaviours. Rather than simply being the brain’s “pleasure chemical,” dopamine has a much more interesting relationship with wanting, anticipation, and reward. Through neuroscience and practical exercises, the book invites readers to better understand the patterns driving their behaviour and find ways to work with their brains rather than against them.

I had the pleasure of chatting with Dr. Ceruto about translating complex neuroscience for everyday readers, the relationship between dopamine and motivation, the surprising ways email and AI can influence our reward systems, and the idea of creating a personalized Dopamine Menu. We also talked about the small changes we can make when we feel overwhelmed and what makes it easier to get started.

I hope you enjoy getting to know Dr. Ceruto and discovering a little more about the science behind our everyday choices!

The Dopamine Code

Goodreads

The Dopamine Code is a game-changer for brains feeling stuck, scattered, or buried under a mountain of to-dos—especially if you’re constantly chasing happiness or struggling with focus and time management. Packed with science on dopamine and how it impacts your motivation and drive, it offers practical strategies like creating dopamine menus to spark joy, reduce overwhelm, and actually get things done without burning out.

Have you ever felt stuck or scattered? Like you’re constantly chasing happiness? The answer is found in the brain chemical that determines how satisfied (or unsatisfied) you dopamine. Discover how to rewire your brain for more happiness, motivation, mental clarity, and productivity with The Dopamine Code!

Blending neuroscientist Dr. Sydney Ceruto’s research with real-life examples, this book covers the basics of dopamine and how you can work with your brain to spark joy, increase focus—including focus for ADHD—and reduce overwhelm through better emotional regulation and energy management. Each chapter contains an exercise to help you apply what you learn to work, relationships, creativity, and more. You’ll also find a guide for creating your own Dopamine Menu—a toolkit of routines and strategies that match your unique strengths, values, and lifestyle.

Whether you’re navigating school, work, or are simply trying to make it through the day without burning out, you’ll find a game-changing blueprint that’s doable, sustainable, and easy to understand. A more motivated and fulfilling life is within reach…it’s time to crack the dopamine code!

Get to know the author: Dr. Sydney Ceruto

Hi Dr. Ceruto! Welcome to Armed with A Book. It is a pleasure to chat with you. Please tell me and my readers a bit about yourself.

The pleasure is mine, Kriti. I’m a neuroscientist and author, with a PhD in behavioral and cognitive neuroscience from NYU and master’s degrees in clinical psychology and business psychology from Yale. I also lecture in Wharton’s Executive Development Program at the University of Pennsylvania.

In 2000 I founded MindLAB Neuroscience, which I still lead. I work with a handful of clients at a time, helping them rewire the patterns that drive their behavior and decisions.

I’m the author of The Dopamine Code, published by Simon & Schuster, and its hands-on companion, The Dopamine Code Workbook.

Away from work, I have a daredevil side, and I’ll try almost anything once. That’s how I ended up in Pamplona, running with the bulls. I’ve picked grapes in Bordeaux, watched the sun go down over the Sahara, and woken up with elephants nearby in Africa. I’m happily married to a wonderful, loving husband, and I’m a very proud mom to our son. I’m crazy about animals, especially my long-haired micro-Chihuahua. I’m also a big sports fan, football and Formula One most of all, and golf has been my long-running lesson in focus and patience.

What first drew you to neuroscience?

I lost both of my parents when I was 15, and for years after that I saw one therapist after another. I’d usually walk out of a session feeling better, but within a few days the panic and despair would come back, along with a fear of abandonment I couldn’t shake. I’d find myself needing an emergency session because I couldn’t hold out until my next weekly appointment. I really believed something in me was broken.

That’s what drew me to behavioral and cognitive neuroscience. I wanted to understand what was happening in my own head. It turned out the panic and the fear of abandonment ran on pathways my brain laid down after I lost my parents and kept firing for years after the loss. Once I could see that, I could work on those pathways and build new ones, and within months the panic was easing.

The Dopamine Code tackles numerous human behaviors and explains how dopamine and the systems around it influence our actions. I had to read this book slowly because there was so much to take in. What was the most challenging part of writing a book about such a complex topic for a general audience?

Translating the science was the hardest part: taking complex neurobiology and turning it into something a general reader could use. My editor, Sarah Doughty, was a huge help there. She cared as much about the science as she did about the reader, and she pushed every chapter until it was clear and practical.

You mentioned reading it slowly, and that’s what I’d recommend to anyone. Every chapter ends with a summary, and nearly every one includes a “Pause and Practice” exercise so you can stop and try something before moving on. The case studies help too. Each one blends the experiences of many clients I’ve worked with.

Was there a particular moment or experience that made you realize there was a need to share what you know about dopamine with a wider audience?

Yes, there were two, and the first was in my own life. On paper I was doing well, raising my son, running a household and building my practice. But I was always craving the next thing, whether it was a shopping spree, a new car or a big vacation. Each one gave me a quick rush, and then the emptiness came back stronger than before. Then we got to dopamine in my graduate neuroscience studies, and something inside me clicked into place. My dopamine pathway had been steering my life the whole time, and I’d been chasing the next high and crashing every time it wore off.

The second came once I saw it in myself and started noticing it in friends, clients and strangers. So many of them felt that same pull toward the next thing, and it took the shine off even their best moments. In my practice, it came up constantly. Their brains were running an ancient reward system in a world full of things designed to set it off. They needed a practical way to work with it.

We often hear dopamine described simply as the “pleasure chemical.” What is one misconception about dopamine that you particularly hope readers will leave behind after reading your book?

That “pleasure chemical” label you mentioned is the one I’d most like readers to let go of. Dopamine is what makes you want something. It rises when you anticipate a reward, and it keeps you moving toward it. The pleasure you feel once you get it runs on different chemicals, including your brain’s natural opioids.

I had a client a couple of years ago who finally reached a goal she’d been chasing for years, and she felt almost nothing. She said she just felt flat, and it bothered her how flat she felt. All the excitement had been in the chase. Once she arrived, she had nothing left to look forward to.

So if you’ve ever felt empty right after getting what you wanted, that’s often just the chase ending. Before you rush on to the next thing, take time to savor what you just got.

One thing I appreciated about the book is that it doesn’t treat motivation as a simple matter of willpower. What do you wish we understood better about the difference between knowing what we want to do and actually being able to initiate it?

You can know exactly what you want to do and still not start, because starting depends on your brain’s wanting system. Dopamine has to fire ahead of time to make a goal feel worth going after. When that system gets flooded all day with easy hits, like your phone and passive scrolling, it goes quiet, and nothing that takes real effort feels worth chasing.

Before you call yourself lazy, look at how many easy rewards are crowding your day. Clearing some of them out can bring the wanting back on its own.

Social media is an obvious example of a system designed around notifications, engagement, and the promise of something new. But email arrived long before social media and can create a surprisingly similar cycle of checking, anticipating, and responding. How do you think everyday tools like email affect the brain’s reward systems?

Email works on the same loop as social media, just more quietly. Most of what lands in your inbox is routine, but every so often something good shows up, like the reply you were hoping for. Since you never know which check will bring it, you keep checking, the same way people keep pulling a slot machine lever.

Answering email also feels productive. Every reply you send gives you a small sense of getting something done, so you can spend an hour in your inbox and still not touch the work you meant to do.

Before you open your inbox, write down your priorities for the day so you start with your own plan. Then save email for the times when your energy is lower, so it doesn’t eat up your best hours.

We are also increasingly interacting with chatbots and AI for conversation, advice, and even emotional support. Because these systems are designed to respond to us and keep us engaged, what questions should we be asking about the relationship between AI and our brains?

The first question I’d ask is what the chatbot is designed for: to help you get something done, or to keep you coming back. If it’s the second, it’s working on your reward system much the way a social feed does.

I’d also ask what it’s taking the place of. Using a chatbot to think something through or do research is fine, but I’d pay attention if it starts replacing a call to a friend. In the book I write about how the brain’s attachment system can confuse availability with intimacy. A chatbot is about as available as anything gets, since it answers instantly and never needs anything back from you.

Emotional support is where I’d be most careful. Chatbots often tell you what you want to hear, and approval feels good because it gives your brain a quick hit of dopamine. But feeling truly understood depends on signals a chatbot can’t give you, like eye contact, touch and body language. In person, those signals bring oxytocin into play, which helps build the trust and sense of safety that make support feel real. After you lean on a chatbot for comfort, notice whether reaching out to a real person feels easier or harder.

The idea of a customizable dopamine menu is one of the book’s most practical tools. How did you arrive at this concept, and what makes a dopamine menu different from another productivity system?

I can’t take credit for the idea itself. The dopamine menu went viral on social media before my book came out. What my book adds is the neuroscience of why it works, and my version took shape as I worked on my own patterns and with my clients.

Where a productivity system tracks what you need to get done, a Dopamine Menu lists the experiences that keep your drive steady. Main courses, like a creative project or learning something that absorbs you, give you the deep focus and sense of progress that recharge your reward system. Side dishes, like a ten-minute walk or a quick call with a friend, are short resets that keep you from getting mentally worn out between stretches of focus. Restorative desserts, like music, time in nature or sleep, are for mental and emotional renewal.

And because you build it from what works for your own brain, it’s personal. Think of it as an experiment and keep adjusting it as your life changes.

What’s on your own dopamine menu?

My main course is golf. I’ve been working on my game for about twenty years, and it still tests my resilience every time I play. Writing is another, usually early in the morning. I’m also a voracious reader, and I’ll pick up anything from the Russian classics to the latest neuroscience papers.

My side dishes are quick. I’ll take Gisella, my Chihuahua, for a walk or let her run along the beach, or catch up on football or Formula One.

For dessert, I keep it simple: watching the sunset and getting a good night’s sleep. And I’ll admit that some nights I go for thoughtless TV, like Real Housewives, or a game on my phone. I just keep it to an episode or a round or two. Every so often I add a special, which is what I call the once-in-a-while items. For me, that’s travel, ideally somewhere I’ve never been.

For someone who feels overwhelmed by their to-do list and doesn’t know where to begin, what is the smallest change you would suggest making?

Pick one thing on your list and break it down until the first step takes just a few minutes. If the task is “finish the report,” the first step might be “open the file and write the first heading.” Do only that, and take a second to notice that it’s done.

Finishing even a small step gives you a sense of progress, which switches on your brain’s reward circuits and makes the next step easier to start.

If you could remove one modern-day source of unnecessary dopamine stimulation from everyone’s lives for a week, what would you choose?

I’d switch off non-essential push notifications. Every buzz pulls you out of what you’re doing, and after enough of them you start picking up your phone even when it hasn’t made a sound.

For one week, turn off everything except calls and messages from the people who matter most. Then notice how often you still reach for your phone, and what makes you do it.

Is there anything else you would like to add?

I’d just add a word about The Dopamine Code Workbook, which turns the ideas into practice. You audit your own wanting and liking, map the hooks that pull at you most, and then build your own Dopamine Menu and test it for two weeks.

And if you recognized yourself in any of this, whether it’s the flat feeling after a win or reaching for your phone without thinking, start with one small change from this conversation and see how your brain responds.

Thank you so much for taking the time to chat with me!

Thank you so much for having me on Armed with A Book, Kriti. I know the book is a lot to take in, and I really appreciate you reading it as slowly and carefully as you did.

Thank you, reader, for joining me and Dr. Ceruto today!
