The Invisible Anniversary

November 16, 2026

Sponsored by
0:00 / 3:43
Instaread

ON NOVEMBER 12, 2001, two months after the 9/11 terrorist attacks, American Airlines flight 587 crashed after takeoff from New York’s JFK Airport killing 265 people. This was, and remains, one of the deadliest airline accidents ever to happen on U.S. soil, second only to the American flight 191 disaster, in Chicago, in 1979.

It also was twenty-five years ago. Since that day, there hasn’t been a single multiple-fatality crash among the U.S.legacy airlines.

For an understanding of what makes this streak so astonishing, one needs only to flip through the accident records of the 1960s, 70s, 80s and 90s. In the old days, for the majors to go so much as a single year without a front page crash was unusual, never mind twenty-five.

To be clear, there have been a number of post-2001 tragedies involving regional carriers and freighters. The worst of these were the collision over the Potomac in 2025 (67 dead), the 2006 Comair disaster at Lexington (50 fatalities), and the Colgan Air crash near Buffalo in 2009 (49 killed). As for the legacies, in 2005 a young boy in a car was killed when a Southwest Airlines 737 overran a runway in Chicago, and in 2018 a woman on a Southwest flight was killed after being partially ejected through a blown-out cabin window.

What we haven’t seen, however, is the kind of large scale crash that was once brutally routine, year after year. Take a look through the archives. Seldom would a year pass without recording one or more mishaps with dozens or even hundreds killed at a time.

In 1974, just to pick one, the U.S. majors recorded five crashes, including two within three days of each other. Years like that were typical, and the idea that we could go a quarter of a century without even one such incident was unthinkable.

I was impressed when we made it to five years, amazed when we made it to ten, startled when we hit 15, and absolutely shocked when we got to twenty.

And still the streak goes on. We’ve grown accustomed to it. Even I have. The November 12th anniversary slipped right past me. I’m publishing this post four days late, after a reminder from a friend.

It’s especially remarkable when you consider there are nearly twice as many planes, carrying twice as many people, as there were in 2001. The streak also takes in those dark years of the early 2000s, when pretty much all of the big carriers were in and out of bankruptcy, fighting for survival. Not to mention challenges brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic. Best of times, worst of times. All it would have taken is one screw-up, one tragic mistake. Yet here we are.

How we got here is mainly the result of better training, better technology, and the collaborative efforts of airlines, pilot groups, and regulators. We’ve engineered away what used to be the most common causes of accidents.

Yes, we’ve been lucky too, and lack of a headline tragedy does not mean we should rest on our laurels. Complacency is about the worst response we could have. Air safety is all about being proactive — even a little cynical. Our air traffic control system needs upgrades, our airports need investment. Terrorism and sabotage remain threats, and regulatory loopholes need closing.

Duly noted, but a congratulatory moment is, for today, well earned. This isn’t a minor story.

Unless I missed something, nowhere in the media has this anniversary been mentioned. Crashes, not an absence of them, make the news. But there’s no overstating it: we just marked one of the most significant milestones in commercial aviation history.
